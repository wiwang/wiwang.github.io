A model can fit its training data almost perfectly and still make unreliable predictions. Another can be impressively stable yet consistently miss the structure of the problem. Bias and variance give us a language for separating those failures, but only if we define them carefully.

**Bias is not training error, and variance is not test error.** They describe properties of a learning procedure across hypothetical training datasets. A single train/test split gives us evidence about generalization; it does not directly reveal either quantity.

This article develops the squared-error decomposition, measures it in a controlled simulation, and uses the result to reason about regularization and ensembles. It closes with the limits of the familiar U-shaped error curve.

## Start with a prediction problem

Assume a regression target generated by

$$
Y = f(X) + \varepsilon,
\qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid X]=0,
\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X=x)=\sigma^2(x).
$$

Here $f(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]$ is the regression function. A learning algorithm maps a training dataset $D$ to a predictor $\hat f_D$. If training is randomized, we can include that randomness in $D$ as well.

Fix an input $x$. Imagine repeatedly collecting a training dataset of the same size from the same process and fitting the same algorithm. The mean prediction is

$$
\bar f(x)=\mathbb{E}_D[\hat f_D(x)].
$$

Two questions now become distinct:

- **Bias:** How far is $\bar f(x)$ from $f(x)$?
- **Variance:** How much does $\hat f_D(x)$ fluctuate around $\bar f(x)$?

A straight-line model fitted to a curved relationship can have substantial bias even with abundant data. A flexible model fitted to a small, noisy sample may change sharply when the sample changes. Stability and correctness are separate properties.

## Derive the error decomposition

Let $Y_0=f(x)+\varepsilon_0$ be an independent future outcome at the fixed input $x$. The expected squared prediction error is

$$
\mathbb{E}_{D,\varepsilon_0}\left[(Y_0-\hat f_D(x))^2\right].
$$

Write the residual as three pieces:

$$
Y_0-\hat f_D(x)
=
\varepsilon_0
+\left(f(x)-\bar f(x)\right)
+\left(\bar f(x)-\hat f_D(x)\right).
$$

When we square and take expectations, the cross terms disappear. The future noise has conditional mean zero and is independent of the training procedure; the last term also has mean zero by construction. We obtain

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_0-\hat f_D(x))^2\right]
=
\underbrace{(\bar f(x)-f(x))^2}_{\text{squared bias}}
+
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_D[(\hat f_D(x)-\bar f(x))^2]}_{\text{variance}}
+
\underbrace{\sigma^2(x)}_{\text{noise}}.
}
$$

Averaging this identity over the deployment input distribution gives population risk. Which distribution we average over matters: a model evaluated on one region of the input space can behave differently elsewhere. The [University of Toronto's derivation](https://www.teach.cs.toronto.edu/~csc311notes/bias_variance_decomposition/bv.html) gives another treatment of the squared-loss result.

Three qualifications are useful:

1. This is a decomposition for **squared-error regression**. Classification accuracy and cross-entropy do not inherit this exact additive formula.
2. The noise floor is relative to the information in $X$. Better measurements or additional predictive features can reduce conditional uncertainty.
3. Bias includes the effects of the model family, estimator, regularization, and optimization procedure. It is not simply a synonym for a small model.

### Statistical bias is not sampling bias

My original intuition for this topic was a survey: asking only friends about their favorite beer is a poor way to estimate an entire population's preferences. That is a useful warning about **sampling bias**, but it is not the same mechanism as estimator bias above.

An unrepresentative sample can shift the training distribution away from deployment. Even a flexible, low-variance estimator can then generalize badly. More data from the same unrepresentative source may leave the problem intact. Model flexibility cannot repair a missing part of the population by itself.

## A controlled experiment: fit the same signal 500 times

Real datasets rarely tell us the true $f(x)$, so they cannot usually expose squared bias directly. A simulation can.

The experiment below uses:

| Setting | Value |
| --- | --- |
| True signal | $f(x)=\sin(\pi x)$ |
| Training inputs | 25 evenly spaced points in $[-1,1]$ |
| Training noise | Independent Gaussian noise, standard deviation 0.25 |
| Repeated fits | 500 noisy target vectors, shared across model degrees |
| Model family | Unregularized polynomial least squares, degrees 1–15 |
| Evaluation grid | 401 evenly spaced points in $[-1,1]$ |
| Random seed | 42 |

**The design is fixed:** the inputs stay constant while the training noise changes. The experiment measures variability conditional on those inputs, not variability from drawing new input locations. A Legendre polynomial basis improves numerical conditioning; it spans the same degree-limited polynomial space.

*Figure 1. Twenty individual fits are shown in each panel; the mean curve uses all 500 fits. The dashed orange line is the known signal. Identical training inputs isolate sensitivity to label noise.*

For predictions $\hat f_1(x),\ldots,\hat f_R(x)$, we estimate the mean and variance by

$$
\bar f_R(x)=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat f_r(x),
\qquad
\widehat V(x)=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\hat f_r(x)-\bar f_R(x))^2.
$$

The denominator is $R$, rather than $R-1$, so the empirical squared-error decomposition holds exactly for the finite collection of predictions. These are Monte Carlo approximations to the population quantities; estimated squared bias itself has finite-repetition error.

## Regularization makes the trade-off explicit

Consider a correctly specified linear model with a fixed design matrix:

$$
y=X\beta+\varepsilon,
\qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0,
\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2 I.
$$

For simplicity, assume the data are centered and there is no separately penalized intercept. Ridge regression minimizes

$$
\|y-Xb\|_2^2+\lambda\|b\|_2^2,
$$

so, for $\lambda>0$,

$$
\hat\beta_\lambda=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty.
$$

Define $A_\lambda=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^TX$. Conditional on $X$,

$$
\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\beta_\lambda)=(A_\lambda-I)\beta,
$$

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta_\lambda)
=\sigma^2(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^TX(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}.
$$

If $X=U\operatorname{diag}(s_j)V^T$, ridge shrinks the least-squares coefficient along direction $v_j$ by

$$
\frac{s_j^2}{s_j^2+\lambda}.
$$

Directions with small singular values are weakly supported by the observed design. They can amplify noise in ordinary least squares; ridge suppresses them more strongly. The reduction in coefficient variance is purchased by shrinking real signal too.

Prediction quality depends on where future inputs put weight. At a new feature vector $x$, coefficient bias becomes prediction bias $x^T(A_\lambda-I)\beta$, and prediction variance is $x^T\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta_\lambda)x$. A coefficient can be unstable in a direction that barely matters for one deployment distribution and matters greatly for another.

This is why selecting $\lambda$ on representative validation data is more useful than choosing it from a slogan such as “more regularization prevents overfitting.”

## Why averaging models can help

Suppose $B$ scalar predictions have equal variance $v$ and equal pairwise correlation $\rho$. The variance of their average is

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat f_b(x)\right)
=\rho v+\frac{1-\rho}{B}v.
$$

If errors are highly correlated, adding models quickly reaches a floor. If predictors respond differently to training noise, averaging can reduce variance substantially. This calculation explains the appeal of combining unstable predictors while also explaining why many nearly identical models offer little extra benefit.

The formula assumes equal variances and correlations at the input under discussion. It does not promise that any ensemble will improve test error: averaging can also preserve systematic bias, and the training or selection procedure may change the members' behavior.

## Diagnose the failure before choosing the remedy

Bias and variance are latent explanations, not labels that can be read off a single metric.

| Observation | Possible explanation | Useful next check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Training and validation error are both high | Insufficient features, an overly restrictive model, undertraining, or noisy targets | Compare a stronger baseline; inspect optimization and label quality |
| Training error is low and validation error is much higher | Overfitting, an unsuitable split, or a distribution mismatch | Audit the split and features; test regularization and more representative data |
| Results change sharply across reasonable training samples | Estimator instability or small effective sample size | Repeat group-aware or temporal splits; examine learning curves |
| Every model fails on the same deployment segment | Missing signal or a shifted population | Evaluate by segment and inspect data coverage |

Cross-validation estimates performance under its splitting assumptions; it does not separately identify bias and variance. A bootstrap can probe resampling sensitivity, but it is not automatically the true sampling distribution of the learning procedure, especially with dependent observations or unstable estimators.

Also distinguish **prediction variance** from **metric uncertainty**. A model may predict stably while its measured test score varies because the test set is small. Repeated training and repeated evaluation answer different questions.

## The U-shaped curve has limits

The decomposition is an identity under its assumptions. A U-shaped relationship between model capacity and test error is a possible pattern, not part of that identity.

[Belkin, Hsu, Ma, and Mandal](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.11118) study *double descent*: error can increase near an interpolation threshold and then decrease again as capacity grows further. This does not make bias and variance irrelevant. It means that parameter count alone does not determine their behavior. The learning rule, regularization, data geometry, and solution selected by optimization also matter.

Do not infer that crossing an interpolation threshold will improve your particular model. The simulation in this article remains in a classical least-squares regime and does not demonstrate double descent.

## What to carry into the next experiment

Use the decomposition to ask precise questions: What changes across training datasets? Which inputs define the target risk? Is the model missing signal, amplifying noise, or encountering a different population?

Then choose an intervention that tests the explanation. Add expressive features for a systematic miss. Try regularization or averaging for instability. Improve data coverage for a population mismatch. Measure the result on a validation design that resembles the intended use.

The objective is lower deployment risk, not the smallest model, the largest model, or the lowest training error.
